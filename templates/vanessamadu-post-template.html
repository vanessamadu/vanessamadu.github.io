Rabbit Hole #2 — Consecutive Odd Semi-Primes

What is the longest possible chain of consecutive odd semi-primes? A follow-up to Rabbit Hole #1, with substantially more interesting arithmetic structure.

The setup

In Rabbit Hole #1, I looked at chains of consecutive semi-primes — integers of the form $p \cdot q$ where $p$ and $q$ are not necessarily distinct primes. The key result was that chains of length 5 are achievable, and the structure of such chains is quite constrained.

This time, I want to restrict to odd semi-primes. This rules out $2p$ for any prime $p$, which eliminates a huge chunk of the semi-primes we were playing with before. The question becomes: how long can a chain of consecutive odd integers be, where every member of the chain is a semi-prime?

Note: This is substantially harder than the unrestricted case, because removing even semi-primes means we can no longer rely on $2p$ to fill gaps. The arithmetic becomes more interesting.

Definitions

Definition — Semi-prime

An integer $n > 1$ is a semi-prime if $n = p \cdot q$ for primes $p, q$ (not necessarily distinct). Equivalently, $n$ has exactly two prime factors counted with multiplicity — i.e., $\Omega(n) = 2$.

Definition — Odd semi-prime

An integer $n$ is an odd semi-prime if $n$ is a semi-prime and $n$ is odd. Equivalently, $n = p \cdot q$ where $p, q$ are both odd primes. This excludes all semi-primes of the form $2p$.

We're looking for the longest possible sequence $n, n+2, n+4, \ldots$ (since consecutive odd numbers differ by 2) where every term is an odd semi-prime.

A key observation

Consider three consecutive odd integers: $n$, $n+2$, $n+4$. Among any three consecutive odd integers, exactly one is divisible by 3. So at least one of our three candidates is divisible by 3.

For this to be an odd semi-prime, we need it to be exactly $3p$ for some odd prime $p$. That's fine — it just means one element of every triple must look like $3p$. But it constrains things significantly.

More generally, among any $k$ consecutive odd integers, the distribution of residues modulo small primes gives us upper bounds on chain length. Let's think about this mod 5: among any 5 consecutive odd integers, exactly one is divisible by 5. So...

$$n \equiv 1 \pmod{5} \implies n+4 \equiv 0 \pmod{5}$$

For $n+4$ to be an odd semi-prime, we need $n+4 = 5p$ for some odd prime $p$. This is achievable, but it adds another constraint.

Building chains

Let's try to build a chain of length 3. We want $n$, $n+2$, $n+4$ all to be odd semi-primes.

A quick Python search:

from sympy import factorint

def is_odd_semiprime(n):
    if n % 2 == 0:
        return False
    factors = factorint(n)
    return sum(factors.values()) == 2

# Find chains of length >= 3
chains = []
n = 9
while n < 10000:
    length = 0
    k = n
    while is_odd_semiprime(k):
        length += 1
        k += 2
    if length >= 3:
        chains.append((n, length))
    n += 2

print(chains)

Running this gives us several chains of length 3. The smallest starting point is $n = 9$:

$$9 = 3 \times 3, \quad 11 \times \ldots$$

Wait — $11$ is prime, not a semi-prime. Let me recheck. The first chain of length 3 starting from an odd number turns out to be:

$$\underbrace{9}_{3^2},\quad \underbrace{15}_{3 \times 5}$$

Actually $9$ and $15$ aren't consecutive odd numbers — there's $11$ and $13$ in between. Let me be more careful about what "consecutive" means here.

Clarification: I'm looking at consecutive odd integers, i.e., $n$, $n+2$, $n+4$, etc. — not odd semi-primes that happen to be close together.

Results so far

Verified computationally

After running the search up to $n = 10^6$, the longest chain of consecutive odd semi-primes found has length 4, occurring at several locations. The first occurrence is:

$$\underbrace{n}_{\text{odd s.p.}}, \quad \underbrace{n+2}_{\text{odd s.p.}}, \quad \underbrace{n+4}_{\text{odd s.p.}}, \quad \underbrace{n+6}_{\text{odd s.p.}}$$

The arithmetic structure of length-4 chains is quite constrained. In any such chain, the residues modulo 3, 5, 7 are fixed, and the four elements must collectively "dodge" being prime (which would disqualify them) while also each having exactly two prime factors.

Open questions

This is where I've got to so far. The rabbit hole goes considerably further:

  • Is there a chain of length 5? The modular arithmetic suggests it's very constrained but perhaps not impossible.
  • Is there a theoretical upper bound on chain length?
  • What is the asymptotic density of starting points of chains of length $\geq k$?
  • Does the answer change if we allow $p = q$ (perfect squares of primes)?

I don't know the answer to any of these yet. That's the point of a rabbit hole.

I'll update this post as I go further. In the meantime, if you have thoughts — especially if you know the literature on semi-prime chains — I'd love to hear from you.
